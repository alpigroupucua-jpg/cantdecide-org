The Planning Fallacy: Why We Always Underestimate How Long Things Will Take

Why humans underestimate time, costs, and difficulties — and how to make more realistic predictions.

Alex, Founder of CantDecide.org August 16, 2026 9 min read

Have you ever started something thinking:

“This will only take a couple of weeks.”

And then somehow...

weeks become months?

A home renovation that takes three times longer than expected.

A work project that keeps getting delayed.

A personal goal that you were sure you would finish “soon.”

The strange part is that this happens even to experienced people.

We are not bad at planning because we lack intelligence.

We are bad at planning because our brains naturally create a more optimistic version of the future.

This is called the planning fallacy — a psychological phenomenon where people consistently underestimate the time, costs, and difficulties involved in completing future tasks.

Before you continue, here is what you will learn:

  • Why your brain creates unrealistic predictions even when you have experience.
  • Why previous mistakes often do not automatically make future predictions better.
  • How successful decision makers use the “outside view” to plan more realistically.
  • Practical methods you can use to stop underestimating important projects.

Because the truth is:

The problem is usually not that we cannot predict the future.

The problem is that we predict it from inside our own story.

Why does the planning fallacy happen?

The planning fallacy was introduced by psychologists Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky while studying how people make predictions about uncertain events.

Their discovery was uncomfortable:

People often imagine the future as if the process will go according to the plan.

They think:

“I know what needs to happen. I know how motivated I am. I know what I want to achieve.”

And then they create a prediction based on the ideal version of reality.

The problem?

Reality contains:

  • unexpected problems
  • interruptions
  • delays
  • changing priorities
  • mistakes
  • information we did not have at the beginning

But when we imagine the future, our brain naturally focuses on the goal.

Not everything that can go wrong along the way.

The planning fallacy happens because we imagine the destination more clearly than the journey.

The inside view: the story your brain creates

One of the biggest reasons behind the planning fallacy is something researchers call the inside view.

The inside view means looking at a project from your own perspective.

You think about:

  • your skills
  • your motivation
  • your intentions
  • your ideal process

This feels logical.

Because you are planning your own project.

But there is a hidden problem.

You are looking at the future through the story of success.

Imagine opening a business.

The inside view says:

“I have a good idea. I will work hard. Customers will like it.”

All of those things might be true.

But the outside view asks a different question:

“How long do businesses like this usually take to reach this stage?”

The outside view is less exciting.

But it is usually more realistic.

Why experience does not always fix the problem

A logical assumption would be:

“If I have done this before, I should become better at estimating.”

Sometimes that happens.

But surprisingly often, experience alone does not remove the planning fallacy.

Why?

Because humans are very good at remembering the destination.

We remember:

  • finishing the project
  • achieving the goal
  • feeling successful
  • the moment everything worked

But we often forget the full cost of getting there.

We forget:

  • waiting time
  • unexpected obstacles
  • revisions
  • distractions
  • small problems that accumulated

Our memory creates a cleaner version of reality.

Then we use that edited version of the past to predict the future.

This creates a strange cycle:

“I know last time it took longer than expected. But this time will probably be different.”

And that sentence is one of the most common ways the planning fallacy survives.

A personal observation from building projects

I noticed this pattern many times while working on projects.

The beginning is usually the easiest part.

The idea is exciting.

The vision is clear.

You can imagine the finished result.

At that moment, your brain naturally focuses on possibilities.

“This should be simple.”

“This should only take a few weeks.”

“This is mostly just execution.”

Then reality starts adding details.

A small technical problem.

A decision that requires more research.

A change in direction.

Something you did not consider at the beginning.

The project was not impossible.

The original prediction was simply created from an incomplete picture.

I think this is one of the most useful lessons from decision science:

The first estimate we create is often not a prediction.

It is a story about how we hope things will happen.

The skill is learning to separate optimism from forecasting.

The connection between planning fallacy and decision making

The planning fallacy is not only about deadlines.

It influences many important decisions.

Career decisions

You underestimate:

  • how long it takes to build a skill
  • how difficult a transition will be
  • how much uncertainty exists

Business decisions

You underestimate:

  • costs
  • delays
  • complexity
  • unexpected problems

Personal goals

You underestimate:

  • how motivation changes
  • how many competing priorities appear
  • how difficult consistency becomes

This is why better decision-making is not only about choosing between options.

It is also about creating realistic expectations about the future.

This connects with How the Brain Makes Decisions, because many of our decisions are shaped by mental shortcuts that help us move quickly but can also distort our predictions.

Planning can also demand many repeated choices. As those choices accumulate, decision fatigue can make the next judgment feel more difficult and encourage shortcuts.

The outside view: how better decision makers think

The most powerful solution to the planning fallacy is called the outside view.

Instead of asking:

“How will I personally complete this?”

Ask:

“How do similar situations usually unfold?”

This small change is powerful.

Imagine you are creating a new website.

The inside view says:

“I know what pages I need. I know what I want to build. I can probably finish quickly.”

The outside view asks:

“How long do similar websites usually take when you include design, technical issues, testing, and revisions?”

The outside view is less exciting.

But it uses reality as evidence.

Good decision makers are not people who are less optimistic.

They are people who know when optimism should not be used as a measurement tool.

How to fight the planning fallacy

You cannot remove uncertainty from life.

But you can make your predictions better.

Here are practical methods.

1. Ask: “What happened in similar situations?”

Before starting an important project, search for comparable examples.

Not:

“How fast can I personally do this?”

Instead:

“How long does this usually take?”

Examples:

Instead of:

“I can learn this skill in three months.”

Ask:

“How long does it usually take beginners to reach this level?”

Instead of:

“I can launch this product quickly.”

Ask:

“What usually delays products like this?”

The goal is not to limit yourself.

The goal is to stop pretending you are the first person in history to attempt something.

2. Add a realistic buffer

Most people create one estimate.

Better decision makers create a range.

Instead of:

“This will take four weeks.”

Try:

“Best case: four weeks.

Realistic case: six weeks.

Difficult case: three months.”

This does not make you pessimistic.

It makes you prepared.

A plan that only works if nothing goes wrong is not really a plan.

3. Separate motivation from prediction

This is a big one.

You can be extremely motivated and still underestimate complexity.

These are two different questions:

“Do I believe I can do this?”

and:

“How long will this realistically take?”

Confidence helps you start.

Realism helps you finish.

4. Create checkpoints

Large projects become dangerous when the only measurement is the final deadline.

Instead of:

“I will finish everything by December.”

Create checkpoints:

“By next Friday, this part should be complete.”

“After one month, I should know whether this approach works.”

“After this stage, I will review the original assumptions.”

This prevents you from discovering problems only at the end.

5. Update your plan when reality changes

Many people make one prediction and then defend it.

But good decisions require updating.

If new information appears, change the plan.

This connects with the Sunk Cost Fallacy.

Sometimes people continue following an unrealistic plan because they already invested time and effort into it.

A better question is:

“Knowing what I know now, would I still choose this path?”

Not:

“How do I make my original prediction come true?”

6. Set a stopping rule for research

Planning and overthinking can become connected.

Sometimes people keep planning because they are trying to eliminate all uncertainty.

That is impossible.

At some point, more research stops improving the decision.

This is where the planning fallacy connects with How to Stop Overthinking a Decision.

A useful rule:

“I will collect enough information to make a reasonable decision, not a perfect prediction.”

Perfect prediction is not the goal.

Better prediction is.

The hidden danger: optimistic plans create future stress

One underestimated deadline might seem harmless.

But repeated unrealistic planning creates a bigger problem.

You constantly feel behind.

You believe you are failing.

But the real issue might be that your original expectations were impossible.

This can create:

  • frustration
  • unnecessary pressure
  • rushed decisions
  • lower quality work

A realistic plan is not less ambitious.

It is more respectful of reality.

The planning fallacy and the paradox of choice

There is another interesting connection.

Sometimes the problem is not only predicting how long something will take.

It is predicting while facing too many possibilities.

Should we choose option A?

Option B?

Maybe option C is better?

More possibilities create more planning complexity.

This connects directly with The Paradox of Choice: Why More Options Can Make Decisions Harder.

When there are too many possible futures, it becomes harder to create a realistic prediction about any of them.

Reducing unnecessary options often improves planning quality.

A simple planning fallacy checklist

Before committing to an important project, ask:

1. What am I assuming will go perfectly?

Write those assumptions down.

2. What usually goes wrong in situations like this?

Look for patterns.

3. How long did similar things actually take?

Use the outside view.

4. What would make my estimate wrong?

Prepare for those situations.

5. When will I review my prediction?

Create checkpoints.

This turns planning from imagination into a decision process.

Final thought

The planning fallacy teaches us something uncomfortable:

Our brains are excellent at creating believable stories about the future.

But believable does not always mean accurate.

The goal is not to become negative.

The goal is not to expect failure.

The goal is to combine ambition with realism.

The best decision makers are not people who always predict correctly.

Nobody does.

They are people who notice when reality disagrees with their prediction — and adjust quickly.

Because a good plan is not the one that looks perfect at the beginning.

A good plan is the one that survives contact with reality.
