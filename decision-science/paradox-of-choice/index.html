The Paradox of Choice: Why More Options Can Make Decisions Harder

Why having more choices can sometimes make us less confident, more likely to postpone a decision, and more tempted to choose whatever feels simplest.

Alex, Founder of CantDecide.org August 12, 2026 13 min read

You open Netflix.

There are thousands of things you could watch.

Ten minutes later, you are still scrolling.

You open a restaurant menu with six dishes and choosing feels easy.

Open another menu with seventy-six dishes and suddenly dinner requires strategic planning.

You want to buy a laptop.

At first, having twenty models available feels like freedom.

Then you discover processors, screen technologies, battery estimates, RAM configurations, ports, warranties, reviews, refresh rates, weight, sales, refurbished versions and three slightly different models whose names appear to have been created by someone falling asleep on a keyboard.

You started with:

“Which laptop should I buy?”

An hour later, the question has somehow become:

“What if model B has a slightly better screen but model C lasts forty minutes longer and model D goes on sale next Thursday?”

More choice was supposed to make the decision better.

Instead, the decision became harder.

This is the idea commonly described as the paradox of choice.

In scientific research, the more precise term is often choice overload: situations in which increasing the number of available options can make choosing harder, reduce confidence or satisfaction, encourage postponement, or change the kind of option people select.

But there is an important detail that tends to disappear from popular discussions:

More choice is not always worse.

Sometimes more options are genuinely useful.

The interesting question is not:

“How many choices are too many?”

It is:

“When does another option stop adding value and start adding work?”

Choice feels like freedom—until it becomes homework

Most of us like having options.

And reasonably so.

Choice allows us to find something closer to what we need.

A person choosing a car may reasonably prefer twenty possibilities over being told:

“Here is your car. It is brown. Congratulations.”

Choice provides flexibility.

It lets people express preferences.

It gives us alternatives when one option is too expensive, too risky or simply unsuitable.

So the problem is not choice itself.

The problem appears when the cost of evaluating the choices begins to compete with the benefit of having them.

Every additional serious option creates additional comparisons.

Is A better than B?

What about C?

Now compare A to C.

Then B to C.

Now option D appears.

And unfortunately D has one feature that none of the others have.

The number of possibilities grows much faster than our enthusiasm for comparing them.

The paradox is not that choice is bad. It is that freedom can quietly turn into workload.

The famous jam experiment

One of the best-known demonstrations came from psychologists Sheena Iyengar and Mark Lepper.

In their 2000 research, shoppers encountered displays offering either a relatively limited or extensive assortment of gourmet jam.

The larger display attracted interest, but shoppers were substantially more likely to actually purchase a jar when choosing from the smaller assortment.

The researchers conducted additional experiments involving chocolates and optional academic assignments, and found situations in which smaller choice sets produced greater motivation or satisfaction than larger ones.

It is a wonderful result because it captures something very recognizable.

We can enjoy looking at possibilities without enjoying the process of choosing between them.

Think about online shopping.

You may happily browse fifty pairs of shoes.

That does not mean you want to seriously evaluate fifty pairs of shoes.

Browsing and deciding are different activities.

But the science became more complicated

This is where the story becomes more interesting.

The jam experiment became famous enough that people began treating it almost like a psychological law:

More options = worse decisions.

But later research did not support such a simple rule.

A 2010 meta-analysis by Benjamin Scheibehenne, Rainer Greifeneder and Peter Todd examined 63 experimental conditions from 50 published and unpublished experiments involving more than 5,000 participants.

The average effect of having more choices was essentially zero.

Some studies found choice overload.

Others found little effect.

And some found advantages from having more options.

So was the original idea wrong?

Not necessarily.

A later meta-analysis by Alexander Chernev, Ulf Böckenholt and Joseph Goodman examined 99 observations involving more than 7,000 participants and reached a more conditional conclusion.

Choice overload becomes more likely under particular circumstances—especially when:

  • the options are difficult to compare,
  • the decision itself is difficult,
  • the person is uncertain about what they want,
  • and the chooser is trying to minimize mental effort.

That distinction matters enormously.

The problem is not simply twenty choices.

It is twenty choices when you do not know what matters.

A personal observation from sales

I spent many years in sales involving purchases large enough that people understandably wanted to make the right choice.

And there was a pattern I saw repeatedly.

A customer would sometimes begin by saying:

“I want to see all the options.”

That sounds sensible.

Sometimes it was sensible.

But there was a point where additional alternatives stopped clarifying the decision.

Option number four answered a question.

Option number five showed a useful difference.

Option number six revealed another possibility.

Then option seven, eight and nine mostly created new reasons to hesitate.

The customer who had initially narrowed the decision to two reasonable choices could suddenly become uncertain about all of them.

And the strange thing was that the products had not become worse.

Their understanding of what they wanted had become less clear.

I gradually learned that the better conversation was often not:

“What else would you like to see?”

It was:

“What are the two or three things that actually matter most to you?”

That question changes the whole decision.

Because the purpose of comparing options is not to discover every difference.

It is to discover the differences that matter to you.

More choice is especially difficult when you do not know what you want

This may be the central lesson of choice-overload research.

In experiments by Alexander Chernev, people who already had clearly articulated preferences were better able to handle larger product assortments.

When people knew their preferred attributes, additional choices could actually help because they increased the chance of finding a close match.

People without clear preferences faced a more complicated task.

They had to decide both:

  1. What do I want?
  2. Which option gives it to me?

Those are two different decisions happening simultaneously.

Imagine choosing a hotel.

If you already know:

  • downtown,
  • under $200,
  • parking included,

then a website showing fifty hotels may be useful.

Filters eliminate most of them immediately.

But suppose you have no clear priorities.

Do you value:

  • location,
  • room size,
  • breakfast,
  • parking,
  • reviews,
  • gym,
  • view,
  • cancellation policy,
  • brand,
  • loyalty points,
  • pool,
  • newer rooms?

Now fifty choices create fifty invitations to reconsider what you care about.

You are no longer just choosing a hotel.

You are constructing your preferences while trying to choose.

That is much harder.

This connects directly with How to Stop Overthinking a Decision: sometimes the cure for endless comparison is not more research. It is deciding what information deserves to matter.

More options can make us choose simplicity instead of quality

There is another fascinating effect.

When choices become complicated, we may not merely postpone the decision.

We can change how we decide.

Iyengar and economist Emir Kamenica examined decisions involving financial options.

In experiments, people presented with larger choice sets became more attracted to alternatives that were simpler and easier to understand.

The researchers also analyzed records involving more than 500,000 employees across 638 institutions and found that retirement plans offering more funds were associated with greater allocation toward simpler money-market and bond funds and less toward equity funds.

The important lesson is not that simple investments are bad.

It is that complexity in the choice environment can affect what type of option feels attractive.

When our brain becomes overloaded, simplicity itself gains value.

You see the same thing outside finance.

Twenty complicated phone plans?

Choose the one called “Unlimited Everything.”

Thirty insurance combinations?

Pick the one labeled “Recommended.”

Seventy menu items?

Order the burger you already understand.

Forty subscription tiers?

Choose the middle one.

This also explains why the design of a choice environment can influence us—a theme closely related to The Invisible Push: How Other People Shape Your Decisions Without You Noticing.

When people become tired of comparing, labels such as:

Most Popular

Recommended

Best Value

can become very powerful.

Sometimes those labels are useful.

Sometimes they simply provide relief from having to decide.

The problem gets worse when the options are difficult to compare

Ten nearly identical options can sometimes be harder than twenty clearly different ones.

Imagine comparing three vacation destinations:

  • a beach holiday,
  • a mountain retreat,
  • a city weekend.

Those differences are obvious.

Now imagine comparing twelve hotels in the same neighborhood with almost identical prices and ratings.

Hotel A: 4.5 stars, breakfast included.

Hotel B: 4.6 stars, no breakfast, larger room.

Hotel C: 4.4 stars, cheaper parking.

Hotel D: 4.7 stars, smaller room.

Hotel E: better location but worse cancellation policy.

Each one wins on something.

Nothing clearly dominates.

This creates what researchers call choice-set complexity—one of the conditions associated with greater choice overload.

The brain must keep multiple attributes active while comparing trade-offs.

That is why two options can sometimes feel easy and six carefully balanced options can feel impossible.

“Best” is a dangerous word

Choice becomes especially difficult when we demand the best possible option.

Suppose you need a good coffee machine.

You could define success:

  • under $400,
  • reliable,
  • makes the drinks you actually use,
  • easy to clean.

Once an option meets those requirements, the decision is manageable.

But change the goal:

“Find the best coffee machine available.”

Now you have a problem.

Best according to whom?

At what price?

For espresso?

Milk drinks?

Reliability?

Repairability?

Speed?

Noise?

Counter space?

Every new review creates another dimension on which your current favorite might lose.

The search can become infinite.

This is where choice overload and analysis paralysis often meet.

More choices create more comparison.

More comparison reveals more trade-offs.

More trade-offs create more uncertainty.

More uncertainty creates more research.

And suddenly you are watching a 38-minute YouTube comparison of coffee grinders at 1:17 in the morning.

The coffee machine has won.

A practical way to escape the paradox of choice

The goal is not to reduce every decision to two options.

It is to reduce unnecessary comparison.

Here is a practical method.

1. Decide what matters before looking at everything

Before opening thirty browser tabs, write your three most important requirements.

For example, if choosing a phone:

  1. Excellent battery life.
  2. Good camera.
  3. Under $800.

Now options that fail an important requirement can disappear.

You are using the market to search for what you want rather than allowing the market to tell you what you should want.

Research on articulated preferences suggests that having a clearer sense of your ideal attributes can make larger assortments easier to navigate.

Choose your criteria before the options start choosing your criteria for you.

2. Separate requirements from bonuses

Create two categories.

Must have

Without this, the option is out.

Nice to have

Useful, but not worth reopening the entire decision.

This prevents a feature you did not care about yesterday from suddenly controlling the decision today.

A heated steering wheel is nice.

If you originally cared about reliability, price and fuel economy, the existence of heated steering wheels should probably not send you back to square one.

3. Eliminate before you compare

People often try to carefully rank twenty options.

Usually you do not need to.

First eliminate everything that clearly fails your requirements.

Twenty options may become seven.

Then remove those with obvious disadvantages.

Seven becomes four.

Only then compare carefully.

The objective is not:

“Which of these twenty is number one?”

It is:

“Which options deserve serious consideration?”

That is a much easier question.

4. Create a shortlist

For ordinary decisions, I personally like reducing the active choice to two or three serious candidates.

That number is a practical rule, not a scientific law.

The point is psychological.

You may know that thirty possibilities exist.

You do not need all thirty sitting at the decision table.

Once an option leaves the shortlist, stop researching it unless genuinely new information appears.

Otherwise rejected choices have an annoying habit of returning from the dead.

5. Use the “meaningful difference” test

When two options are extremely close, ask:

“Will I actually notice this difference after I make the decision?”

Laptop A has 13 hours of battery.

Laptop B has 13 hours and 25 minutes.

If everything else is similar, the difference may be technically real but practically irrelevant.

A decision can become difficult because we treat every measurable difference as a meaningful difference.

They are not the same thing.

6. Stop comparing once one option meets the important standard

This does not mean choosing carelessly.

It means having a stopping rule.

For example:

“I will choose once I find an option that meets all three essential criteria, has no major warning signs, and is reasonably priced.”

Without a stopping rule, every acceptable choice creates the temptation to search for a slightly better one.

That search may never end.

This is another place where our article on How to Stop Overthinking a Decision becomes useful: the purpose of research is to improve the decision, not postpone responsibility indefinitely.

7. Ask whether the decision is reversible

Some decisions deserve extensive comparison.

Others do not.

If the decision can easily be reversed, excessive optimization is usually expensive.

Trying a new restaurant?

Choose.

Testing a software tool with a free trial?

Choose.

Buying inexpensive headphones with a return policy?

Choose.

Moving across the country?

Take more time.

Signing a long-term financial contract?

Take more time.

The amount of thinking should roughly match the consequences and reversibility of the decision.

8. Beware of reopening the choice after deciding

Choice overload does not necessarily end when you buy something.

You can create it again afterward.

You purchase a laptop.

Then you continue watching laptop reviews.

You book a hotel.

Then you keep checking competing hotels.

You accept a job.

Then you keep reading job postings.

Now every alternative becomes evidence that something else might have been better.

Unless new information could reasonably require action, stop shopping after you have bought.

A decision cannot become peaceful if you keep inviting the rejected options back into the room.

This also connects with The Sunk Cost Fallacy, although the problems are different.

Sunk cost is about letting past investment control the future.

Choice overload is about allowing too many possible futures to control the present.

Both can trap us between what we already chose and what we might have chosen instead.

The five-question choice reset

When too many options are making you freeze, answer these:

1. What are the three things I actually care about?

Not what the advertisement says matters.

Not what reviewers obsess over.

What matters to you?

2. Which options clearly fail one of those requirements?

Remove them.

3. Which differences between the remaining options would I genuinely notice?

Ignore distinctions that are technically interesting but practically meaningless.

4. Is this decision reversible?

If yes, reduce the amount of analysis.

5. What information could genuinely change my choice?

Research only that.

If you cannot identify information that would change the decision, you may already have enough information.

Sometimes more choice really is better

This deserves repeating because popular psychology often turns nuanced research into slogans.

More choice is not inherently harmful.

If you know exactly what you want, more options may increase your chance of finding it.

If products are easy to compare, a larger assortment can be useful.

If the decision is enjoyable rather than burdensome, exploring options may itself have value.

And when existing options do not meet your needs, additional choice is obviously beneficial.

The scientific literature does not support a universal threshold like:

“Seven choices are good and eight choices destroy the human brain.”

The 2010 meta-analysis found enormous variation between studies. The later 2015 meta-analysis suggests that the conditions surrounding the choice help explain why overload appears strongly in some situations and weakly in others.

So the practical lesson is not:

“Always choose from fewer options.”

It is:

“Reduce the options you need to seriously evaluate.”

Those are very different ideas.

Final thought

We often imagine that the perfect decision exists somewhere inside enough information.

If we compare enough products, read enough reviews, ask enough people and open enough tabs, eventually one choice will emerge glowing from the screen:

THIS ONE.

Reality is rarely so cooperative.

Years in sales taught me that people sometimes become less certain not because the options are bad, but because too many reasonable options remain alive at once.

There is always another alternative.

Another review.

Another price.

Another opinion.

Another possibility.

At some point, the skill is no longer finding more choices.

The skill is knowing which choices deserve your attention.

That is what the paradox of choice really means to me.

Freedom requires options.

But making a decision requires letting most of them go.

So when you feel overwhelmed by possibilities, do not ask:

“Have I considered everything?”

You almost certainly have not.

Ask:

“Have I considered enough to make a responsible choice?”

If the answer is yes, close the tabs.

The decision is waiting.
