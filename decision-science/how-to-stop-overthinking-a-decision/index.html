How to Stop Overthinking a Decision: The Science of Analysis Paralysis

Learn why careful thinking can turn into an endless search for certainty—and how to reach a responsible decision without knowing absolutely everything.

Alex, Founder of CantDecide.org August 2, 2026 11 min read

You have already compared the options.

You have read the reviews, watched the videos, calculated the costs, and asked other people what they would do.

You are still not ready.

So you open another tab.

Maybe one more article will reveal something important. Maybe another opinion will make the correct choice obvious. Maybe tomorrow you will finally feel certain.

But tomorrow arrives, and the decision has not become easier.

It has become heavier.

This is what people often call analysis paralysis: thinking so extensively about a decision that the thinking begins to prevent action.

The solution is not to stop thinking.

The solution is to recognize when thinking has stopped producing useful information.

Thinking is useful while it improves the decision. Once it only repeats the fear, it has changed jobs.

A personal observation

I spent many years working in sales, in an industry where people made one of the largest purchases of their lives after buying a home.

I saw people approach important decisions in very different ways.

Some moved too quickly. They became emotionally attached to an option before properly understanding the costs or alternatives.

Others did the opposite.

They asked for another comparison, another calculation, another variation, and another explanation. Sometimes that additional information genuinely helped.

But sometimes it appeared that the person was no longer searching for information.

They were searching for a guarantee.

They wanted to know that they would never regret the purchase, never discover a better option, and never look back and wonder whether they had made a mistake.

No salesperson, scientist, friend, or website can provide that guarantee.

A responsible decision can reduce uncertainty.

It cannot eliminate uncertainty from life.

Overthinking is not the same as thinking carefully

“Overthinking” is an everyday word, not one single scientific diagnosis.

In research, parts of what people describe as overthinking may be studied as:

  • rumination,
  • repetitive thought,
  • uncertainty,
  • decision deferral,
  • maximizing,
  • choice overload,
  • difficulty committing to action.

Repetitive thought is not automatically harmful.

Psychologist Edward Watkins reviewed evidence showing that repeated thinking can be constructive when it supports planning, preparation, and problem-solving. It becomes less constructive when it remains abstract, repetitive, and disconnected from action.

Compare these two questions:

“Why do I always make bad decisions?”

and:

“What information do I need before Friday, and where can I find it?”

The first question is broad, personal, and almost impossible to finish answering.

The second question is specific and connected to an action.

Both involve thinking.

Only one is moving the decision forward.

Why more thinking can produce less certainty

It sounds logical that thinking longer should make you more confident.

Sometimes it does.

Research on rumination suggests that repetitive thinking can become unhelpful when it interferes with problem-solving and purposeful action.

A 2020 review by Edward R. Watkins and Henrietta Roberts concluded that rumination can obstruct problem-solving and instrumental behavior and reduce sensitivity to changing circumstances.

That does not mean every repeated thought is harmful. The review concerns rumination as a persistent pattern, not every moment of careful reflection.

One way that unproductive cycle can feel during a decision is:

  1. You feel uncertain.
  2. You think more to remove the uncertainty.
  3. The additional thinking reveals more possible problems.
  4. You feel even less certain.
  5. You return to step two.

The mind quietly changes the standard.

At first, you wanted to make a sensible decision.

Now you are demanding a decision that cannot possibly go wrong.

A good decision and a guaranteed outcome are not the same thing.

Rumination can feel productive

This is one reason overthinking is difficult to interrupt.

It feels responsible.

You are not avoiding the decision, you tell yourself. You are researching it.

You are being careful.

You are preparing.

But rumination can imitate problem-solving while delaying the behavior that would actually resolve the situation.

A 2020 review by Edward R. Watkins and Henrietta Roberts concluded that rumination can prolong negative moods, interfere with problem-solving and purposeful behavior, and make it harder to respond to changing circumstances.

That review does not treat every repeated thought as harmful. Watkins’s earlier review emphasizes that the consequences depend on whether thought stays abstract and repetitive or becomes concrete and connected to problem-solving.

A useful test is:

What is different now because of the last thirty minutes of thinking?

Did you discover a new fact?

Did you identify a risk?

Did you remove an unsuitable option?

Did you define your priorities?

Did you create a next step?

Or did you replay the same possibilities using slightly different words?

If nothing changed, you may no longer be analyzing the decision.

You may be circling it.

Too many choices are not always the problem

You have probably heard that more options automatically make people less satisfied.

The science is more complicated.

A 2010 meta-analysis examined 63 experimental conditions and found that the average effect of additional choice was close to zero, with substantial differences between studies.

In some situations, more options created problems. In others, they helped or made little difference.

A later meta-analysis identified several circumstances in which choice overload is more likely:

  • the options are complicated,
  • the decision task is difficult,
  • the person is uncertain about their preferences,
  • the person is trying to minimize the effort required to choose.

This gives us a practical lesson.

The problem is not simply that twenty options exist.

The problem may be that you do not yet know what you are comparing them on.

Imagine choosing a laptop from twenty models.

If you do not know what matters to you, every specification appears important.

Screen size.

Battery life.

Processor speed.

Weight.

Storage.

Price.

Ports.

Brand.

Repairability.

Reviews.

Resale value.

The decision becomes a cloud of unrelated information.

But once you decide that you need a lightweight computer with long battery life below a specific price, many options become irrelevant.

You did not solve the problem by learning everything about every laptop.

You solved it by defining what matters.

More information is not always more clarity. Sometimes clarity comes from deciding which information deserves to matter.

The search for the “best” choice

One influential line of research distinguishes between maximizing and satisficing.

A maximizer tries to identify the best possible option.

A satisficer defines what would meet the important requirements and accepts an option that reaches that standard.

In a 2007 study, Andrew M. Parker, Wändi Bruine de Bruin, and Baruch Fischhoff found that people reporting stronger maximizing tendencies also reported greater regret, more decision avoidance, greater dependence on others when choosing, and less behavioral coping.

These were associations based on self-reports, not proof that maximizing causes those outcomes.

This does not mean that ambition is bad or that you should choose carelessly.

Later research has also debated exactly how maximization should be measured.

But the distinction describes a real practical problem:

If your goal is to find a good option, you can stop when your important criteria are satisfied.

If your goal is to prove that no better option exists anywhere, your research may have no natural endpoint.

A better offer might appear next week.

Another product might be released next month.

Another job might exist at a company you have not discovered.

You can compare the available options.

You cannot compare your choice with every possibility that might ever exist.

Not every decision deserves the same amount of thought

Buying a house deserves more analysis than choosing lunch.

Leaving a career deserves more analysis than selecting a movie.

An irreversible decision deserves more care than something you can test for a week and change.

Yet people often give small decisions enormous amounts of attention while postponing larger decisions because they feel emotionally uncomfortable.

Before researching, classify the decision.

Low stakes and reversible

Choose reasonably quickly.

Examples might include trying a restaurant, buying an inexpensive household item, or testing a new routine.

Meaningful but adjustable

Set criteria, compare a manageable shortlist, and select an option you can review later.

Examples might include software, a course, a gym, or a subscription.

High stakes or difficult to reverse

Use more time, independent advice, and careful verification.

Examples might include major financial commitments, medical decisions, relocation, or contracts with lasting consequences.

This does not produce a scientifically perfect formula.

It prevents you from spending the same mental energy on every choice.

A practical method for stopping analysis paralysis

Here is a decision process you can actually use.

1. Write the exact decision

Do not write:

“What should I do?”

Write:

“Do I accept this offer, reject it, or request different terms?”

A clear decision has defined actions.

A vague decision invites unlimited thought.

2. List your three most important criteria

Not fifteen.

Start with three.

For example:

  • the total cost must remain below a specific amount,
  • the option must meet the main practical need,
  • the risk must remain acceptable.

Three is a practical constraint, not a scientifically magical number.

The purpose is to force prioritization.

You can include additional details later, but first identify the factors capable of changing the decision.

3. Separate facts, predictions, and fears

Create three columns.

Facts

What do you actually know?

Predictions

What do you believe will probably happen?

Fears

What are you afraid might happen?

All three matter, but they are not the same kind of information.

For example:

  • Fact: The new job pays more.
  • Prediction: The work may create better opportunities.
  • Fear: I might fail and regret leaving.

A fear should not be ignored.

But it should not quietly disguise itself as a confirmed fact.

4. Ask what information could change your choice

Do not ask:

“What else can I learn?”

There is almost always something else you can learn.

Ask:

“What missing information could realistically change my decision?”

That question creates a stopping point.

Perhaps you need to confirm the final cost.

Perhaps you need to read the cancellation clause.

Perhaps you need an independent inspection.

But if another hour of reviews would not change what you choose, it is no longer decision-critical research.

5. Reduce the active options

You do not need to erase every alternative from existence.

You only need a manageable final set.

Remove any option that fails an essential criterion.

Then compare the remaining options directly.

A personal rule I find useful is to reduce an ordinary decision to two or three serious candidates.

Again, this is not a universal law.

It is a way to prevent ten weak possibilities from receiving the same attention as two strong ones.

6. Set a stopping rule before continuing

A stopping rule defines when research ends.

For example:

“I will compare these three options, verify the total cost, and decide by Thursday evening.”

Or:

“I will speak with two qualified professionals. Unless they identify a new material risk, I will choose on Monday.”

Without a stopping rule, anxiety can always request one more piece of information.

The goal is not to force an uninformed choice.

It is to define what “informed enough” means before fear begins moving the finish line.

7. Turn the decision into an if–then action

Deciding and acting are different problems.

Research on implementation intentions has found that specifying when, where, and how an action will occur can improve the likelihood that intentions become behavior.

The usual format is:

“If situation X occurs, then I will perform action Y.”

For example:

“If the inspection is acceptable, I will send my final answer by 4:00 p.m.”

Or:

“If I have not discovered a deal-breaking fact by Friday, I will choose the option that best meets my three criteria.”

The plan reduces the need to reopen the entire decision at the final moment.

The 15-minute decision reset

When you catch yourself circling, try this:

Minutes 1–3

Write the exact decision.

Minutes 4–6

Write your three essential criteria.

Minutes 7–9

List the information still missing.

Minutes 10–12

Remove options that fail the criteria.

Minutes 13–15

Set the deadline and the next physical action.

At the end, you do not need to feel perfectly certain.

You need to know what happens next.

Movement does not require complete certainty. It requires a decision that is responsible enough to act on.

After deciding, stop holding the trial again

Many people make a decision and then immediately reopen it.

They keep checking the rejected options.

They search for evidence that they made a mistake.

They compare the real imperfections of their chosen option with the imaginary perfection of the alternatives.

Unless new material information appears, give the decision a closed period.

For example:

“I will not reconsider this for thirty days.”

You can schedule a review if the decision is adjustable.

That is different from questioning it every evening.

A review has a date and criteria.

Rumination has no appointment. It simply keeps knocking.

When overthinking is trying to protect you

Overthinking is not always an enemy.

It may be attempting to protect you from:

  • regret,
  • embarrassment,
  • financial loss,
  • criticism,
  • responsibility,
  • discovering that you were wrong.

That protective instinct deserves respect.

But it should not receive unlimited authority.

You can acknowledge the risk and still choose.

You can make a careful decision and still experience an imperfect outcome.

You can regret part of a choice without proving that the choice was irrational.

A decision should be judged by the information and reasoning available when you made it, not only by whether luck later cooperated.

Final thought

The purpose of decision-making is not to achieve a permanent state of certainty.

It is to choose a responsible direction using the information you can reasonably obtain.

Years in sales taught me that people are not always missing another fact.

Sometimes they are waiting for someone to remove the emotional responsibility of choosing.

But nobody else can fully do that for us.

We can seek evidence.

We can ask for advice.

We can slow down.

We can protect ourselves from pressure.

Eventually, however, we have to accept that choosing one path means releasing the others.

That is not a failure of analysis.

That is what a decision is.

When you notice yourself thinking in circles, stop asking:

“How can I become completely certain?”

Ask:

What would I need to know to make this decision responsibly—and do I already know it?

If the answer is yes, the next step is probably not more thinking.

It is action.
